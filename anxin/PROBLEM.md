# The problem

## Where this started

Monash Malaysia is a genuinely mixed campus, with a large Chinese-speaking
community among the students. One of us is an international student who
spends a lot of time on WeChat with Chinese friends — and constantly runs
into two problems that, from the inside, feel like the same problem.

The first is not getting the joke. Memes, slang, and references go past
without landing. There is no obvious way to ask what something means
without admitting you didn't understand it, which is a small
embarrassment, but a real one, and it happens often enough that you stop
asking.

The second is not being able to tell whether something is *safe*. A
forwarded message arrives and you cannot judge it. Is this a real
promotion, a real notice from a bank, a real news story? Or is it a scam
that everyone else in the group chat can already smell? Asking has the
same social cost as asking about the joke — and there is a second cost on
top: checking a suspicious message usually means pasting a private
conversation into some website you know nothing about.

So people do what we did: nothing. They scroll past the joke and they
guess about the message.

## The version that actually hurts

Being confused by a meme is annoying. Being unable to evaluate a scam is
dangerous — and it isn't dangerous for us. We don't click shady links.
Our parents and grandparents might.

Our teammate Lip lives the sharper version of this. His elders text and
speak in Chinese. He mainly reads and writes English. When a scam message
reaches them, he is the person best placed to spot it — he's younger, more
online, more familiar with how these things work. But by the time the
message reaches him, the parts that would give it away are gone. Tone,
urgency, the specific wording that makes a phishing text obvious — that is
exactly what does not survive being half-translated across a generation
and a language.

The result is a strange gap. The family's safety net exists. It just
can't read the message.

## What we think the real problem is

The people most exposed to scams and the people best equipped to catch
them are often **separated by a language barrier inside their own family**.

Tools exist on both sides of that barrier. What is missing is something
that speaks to both at once, about the same message, with the same
evidence — so a parent reading Chinese and an adult child reading English
can look at one answer together and act on it.

And it has to be usable by someone who is embarrassed, worried, or
in a hurry:

- **No account.** Nobody signs up to check whether a text is a scam.
- **No stored messages.** People are checking private conversations.
- **No lecture.** Nobody should feel foolish for asking.
- **No confident guess.** If it can't tell, it should say so — an
  authoritative-sounding wrong answer is worse than no answer, because
  the whole point is that this person already can't judge for themselves.

That last one matters most. The audience for this tool is, by
definition, people who cannot check the answer they are given. Anything
that sounds more certain than it is exploits exactly the vulnerability it
claims to protect.
